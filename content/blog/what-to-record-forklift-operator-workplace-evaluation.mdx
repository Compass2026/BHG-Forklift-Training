## Why does a forklift operator workplace evaluation matter?

When an employer puts someone on a powered industrial truck, the question is not only whether the person sat through a class. It is whether the operator has been trained and evaluated well enough to work on their own. Under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.178(l), employers must ensure powered industrial truck operators complete appropriate training and evaluation before they operate independently, subject to the standard’s exceptions. You can read the [full text of the OSHA powered industrial truck standard](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178) on OSHA's website.

That sentence puts real weight on the evaluation. A good record of it shows what was observed, who observed it and which truck was involved. A thin record leaves everyone guessing later. This article walks through what employers can sensibly write down when they evaluate an operator in their own workplace, and how that record connects to the classroom side of a forklift training course.

## What should the evaluation record identify first?

Start with the basics that make the record usable months later:

- The operator's name and the date of the evaluation.
- The name of the person who carried out the evaluation.
- The type of truck the operator used during the evaluation.
- The area of the workplace where the evaluation took place.

The truck type deserves attention. A sit-down electric rider truck, a narrow aisle truck and an electric pallet jack do not handle the same way. Training for one is not automatically a record for another, so write down which truck the operator actually drove. If your operators work on more than one kind of truck, keep a separate line for each.

## What operating tasks should an employer observe and record?

An evaluation is an observation of real work. Write down the tasks the operator performed and how the operator handled them. Useful categories include:

- **Pre-use checks.** Did the operator inspect the truck before starting work, and what did they do when something looked wrong?
- **Travel.** How did the operator drive in the actual aisles, around corners, near pedestrians and across any ramps or uneven surfaces in your building?
- **Load handling.** How did the operator pick up, carry, stack and set down loads that match what your workplace really moves?
- **Parking and shutdown.** Did the operator park the truck properly and secure it when leaving it?

Keep the notes plain. "Slowed at the blind corner and sounded the horn" tells a reader far more than "good driving." Notes that describe behaviour are easier to review and easier to defend than a single word of approval.

## Why does the workplace itself belong in the record?

The same operator can drive well in an open lot and struggle in a crowded warehouse. That is why the workplace conditions matter to the evaluation. Note the conditions the operator worked in: the layout, the traffic from other trucks and people, the surfaces and the kinds of loads. If the operator will work in several parts of a facility, evaluate and record more than one of them.

Conditions also change. New racking, a rearranged floor or a different product can alter what safe operation looks like. A record that names the conditions at the time of the evaluation helps you see when it is time to look again.

## When should an employer evaluate an operator again?

Evaluation is not a one-time event. OSHA 29 CFR 1910.178(l)(4) identifies unsafe operation, incidents or near misses, unsafe evaluations, different truck types and changing workplace conditions as reasons for relevant forklift refresher training. Each of those is a prompt for the employer to look again at an operator.

In practice, that means your records should make these triggers easy to spot. Consider noting:

- Any observed unsafe operation and what was done about it.
- Any incident or near miss involving the operator, and the follow-up.
- A change to a different truck type.
- A meaningful change in the workplace conditions.

If an evaluation itself shows unsafe operation, record that honestly. The point of the record is not to produce a clean page. It is to show that the employer noticed a problem and responded to it.

## How does the workplace evaluation fit with formal instruction?

Evaluation in your own facility sits alongside instruction, not in place of it. Classroom-style learning covers the concepts, and the workplace evaluation shows how the operator applies them on your floor. Employers who are lining up instruction for operators can look at the [Forklift Operator Certification](https://www.bhgforklifttraining.com/classes) page for the classes BHG Forklift Training offers.

Because truck types differ, it helps to match instruction to the equipment your operators use. BHG Forklift Training describes its classes by truck type, including [Class I Electric Rider Forklift Training](https://www.bhgforklifttraining.com/classes/class-1-forklift), [Class II Narrow Aisle Forklift Training](https://www.bhgforklifttraining.com/classes/class-2-forklift) and [Class III Electric Pallet Jack Training](https://www.bhgforklifttraining.com/classes/class-3-forklift). Looking at the class that matches your equipment makes it easier to see what your operators should be ready to demonstrate during your own evaluation.

## What does a practical evaluation checklist look like?

A simple form your supervisors can complete in a few minutes beats a long one that nobody finishes. A workable layout has these parts:

- **Operator and evaluator details.** Names, date and workplace area.
- **Truck details.** The type of truck used.
- **Observed tasks.** Pre-use check, travel, load handling, parking, each with a short note on what was seen.
- **Conditions.** Layout, traffic, surfaces and loads at the time.
- **Outcome.** Whether the operator may work independently, and any follow-up that was agreed.
- **Signatures.** The evaluator and the employer's representative.

Keep the completed forms where a manager can find them. If an operator changes trucks, has an incident or moves to a different part of the building, pull the earlier record out and compare it with the new circumstances.

## What are common mistakes to avoid?

- **Recording an outcome with no observations.** A tick in a box without notes does not show what was seen.
- **Ignoring the truck type.** An evaluation on one truck does not describe an operator's work on another.
- **Skipping the conditions.** A record that never mentions the workplace cannot show whether it still applies.
- **Hiding problems.** If an evaluation turns up unsafe operation, write it down and say what happens next.
- **Treating the record as finished forever.** Revisit it when the triggers above appear.
