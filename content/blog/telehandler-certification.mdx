A telehandler looks more like a small crane than a warehouse forklift. Its boom reaches up and out, it rides on big pneumatic tires, and it spends its days on job sites and farms rather than on smooth concrete. So it's fair to ask whether a telehandler needs different certification from a forklift.

In OSHA's eyes, a telehandler *is* a forklift. What changes is how much the truck-specific training matters.

## Is a telehandler a forklift?

Yes. Telehandlers, also called variable-reach or telescopic forklifts, are powered industrial trucks under OSHA's standard, [29 CFR 1910.178](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178). They belong to the rough terrain group that OSHA's [Powered Industrial Trucks eTool](https://www.osha.gov/etools/powered-industrial-trucks/types-fundamentals/types) lists as Class VII: Rough Terrain Forklift Trucks. That's the same class as straight-mast rough terrain forklifts.

On construction sites, the construction standard points straight back to the general industry rule. [29 CFR 1926.602(d)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.602) says its operator training requirements "are identical to those set forth at §1910.178(l)." So whether a telehandler is setting trusses on a building site or loading trucks in a lumber yard, the same operator training rule applies. (Agricultural operations are regulated separately, under OSHA's agriculture standards, and 1910.178 itself excludes farm vehicles. Farms using telehandlers should check which rules apply to them, though the training topics below are just as relevant there.)

## What telehandler certification requires

Because telehandlers fall under the powered industrial truck rule, certification works the way it does for any forklift. Under 1910.178(l), each operator must complete:

1. **Formal instruction** on the truck and the workplace
2. **Practical, hands-on training** on the type of truck they'll operate
3. **An evaluation of their performance in the workplace**

The employer then certifies each operator, recording the operator's name, the dates of training and evaluation, and who did them. Operators are re-evaluated at least every three years.

The catch is the phrase *type of truck*. Training has to cover the operating instructions, controls and hazards of the truck the operator will actually use. An operator certified on a sit-down warehouse forklift isn't automatically ready for a telehandler, and moving an operator to a different type of truck is one of the triggers for refresher training in 1910.178(l)(4).

## What makes telehandler training different

A telehandler does the same basic job as any forklift, lifting and placing loads. But extending the boom changes nearly everything about how the machine behaves. Good telehandler training spends most of its time on these topics:

### Load charts

A warehouse forklift has one capacity on its data plate. A telehandler's capacity changes with boom angle and extension. The same machine that lifts its full rated load close in may manage only a fraction of it with the boom fully extended. Operators need to read the load chart for the attachment in use and check it before every lift, not just the first one of the day. OSHA's rule is simple: only loads within the rated capacity of the truck may be handled (1910.178(o)(2)).

### Stability on uneven ground

Telehandlers work on slopes, soft ground, gravel and mud. Frame leveling, stabilizers or outriggers (where fitted), and keeping the boom low while traveling all affect whether the machine stays upright. Training should practice leveling, then lifting only when the machine is level and stable.

### Attachments

Forks, buckets, carriages, jibs and work platforms each change the machine's capacity and behavior, and each has its own load chart. OSHA requires manufacturer approval for modifications and additions that affect capacity and safe operation. It also requires the capacity and instruction plates to be updated to match (1910.178(a)(4)), and non-factory front-end attachments must be marked on the truck (1910.178(a)(5)). A truck carrying an attachment is operated as a partially loaded truck even when it isn't handling a load (1910.178(o)(4)).

### Suspended loads

Hanging a load from a hook or winch attachment changes the rules. OSHA's crane standard for construction excludes forklifts *except* "when configured to hoist and lower (by means of a winch or hook) and horizontally move a suspended load" ([29 CFR 1926.1400(c)(8)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1400)). A telehandler used that way on a construction site can fall under the crane rules as well as the forklift rules. Check before you rig anything.

### Lifting people

Never lift anyone on the forks or on a pallet. If a telehandler will be used to lift people, use only a work platform the telehandler's manufacturer has approved for that model, and follow the manufacturer's instructions for it, including its load chart.

### Visibility and the boom

The boom blocks a large part of the operator's view to one side, and long or high loads block more. Training should cover using a spotter, traveling with the load low, and watching for overhead power lines, which a fully raised boom can reach.

### Wind and weather

A raised load on an extended boom acts like a sail. Manufacturers publish wind limits for their machines, and operators should know them. Rain, mud and frost also change traction and stopping distance on a job site.

## Can you get telehandler certified online?

Only the classroom part. As with any forklift, online modules can deliver formal instruction, but OSHA also requires hands-on training and a workplace evaluation on the truck itself. For a telehandler, that practical work is where the risk lies: reading a real load chart, leveling on a real slope, placing a load at full extension. ## Certification checklist for employers

- ☐ Operator meets the federal age rules (18 or older for non-agricultural work)
- ☐ Formal instruction covers telehandler controls, load charts, stability, attachments and your site's hazards
- ☐ Hands-on training on the model, or type, the operator will use
- ☐ Evaluation on your site, with the attachments the operator will use
- ☐ Written certification: name, training date, evaluation date, trainer and evaluator
- ☐ Re-evaluation at least every three years, and sooner after unsafe operation, an incident, a new type of truck or changed conditions
- ☐ Daily pre-shift inspection. See our [forklift inspection checklist](/blog/forklift-inspection-checklist); for a telehandler, add the boom, the attachment and its locking pins, the stabilizers and the load chart
