Search for forklift certification and the first page is full of offers like "Get certified online in 60 minutes" or "Print your card today." Some of these courses are well made. But an online course on its own doesn't make an operator certified under OSHA's rules, and employers who treat it as the whole job are exposed.

Here's what online training can do, what it can't, and how to use it properly.

## What OSHA requires

OSHA's powered industrial truck standard, [29 CFR 1910.178(l)(2)(ii)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-1910.178), says operator training must consist of a **combination** of three things:

1. **Formal instruction**, such as lecture, discussion, interactive computer learning, video or written material
2. **Practical training**, meaning demonstrations by the trainer and practical exercises performed by the trainee
3. **Evaluation of the operator's performance in the workplace**

"Interactive computer learning" is named right there in the first item, so online courses are a legitimate way to deliver formal instruction. The second and third items are the problem. Neither can happen through a screen.

## What an online course can cover

A good online module can teach the material every operator needs to know before touching a truck:

- Truck controls, instruments and warning devices
- The stability triangle and why forklifts tip
- Reading the data plate and understanding load capacity
- Pre-use inspection
- Fueling and battery charging procedures
- Pedestrian safety and general rules of the road

For employers with shift workers spread across several sites, that's useful. It means less time with everyone in a room, and a consistent baseline across the crew.

## What it can't cover

**Practical training.** The trainee has to operate the truck, under the direct supervision of someone qualified to train them. A video of someone else driving doesn't count.

**The workplace evaluation.** OSHA requires an evaluation of each operator's performance *in the workplace*. That means a qualified person watching the operator handle real loads, on the truck they'll use, in the aisles, docks and yards where they work.

**Your specific trucks and site.** The standard requires training on the operating instructions and hazards of the particular type of truck, and on the conditions of your workplace: surfaces, ramps, narrow aisles, pedestrian traffic, ventilation. A generic course written for every warehouse in the country can't know that your dock plates are steep or that your cold-storage floor gets wet at the freezer door.

## The certificate problem

Many online providers issue a wallet card or printable certificate at the end. That document may show the operator finished a course, which is fine. But under 1910.178(l)(6), certification is something the **employer** does after training *and* evaluation, and it records the dates of both and who performed them. A card from a website that never saw the operator drive can't fill that role on its own.

This matters most after an incident. If an operator is hurt or hurts someone, one of the first questions an OSHA compliance officer will ask is whether that operator was trained and evaluated on that truck in that workplace. "They watched a course online" isn't an answer to that question.

## Questions to ask before you buy an online course

If you're considering an online course for the classroom portion, ask the provider:

- **Does the course cover the specific truck types we run?** A course built around sit-down counterbalance trucks won't prepare someone for an order picker or a telehandler.
- **What does the certificate actually say?** Look for wording that makes clear it documents *formal instruction only*, not a complete certification.
- **Who does the hands-on training and evaluation?** If the answer is "you do," make sure someone at your site has the knowledge, training and experience OSHA requires to train and evaluate operators.
- **What records will we get?** You'll need dates and names to build each operator's certification record, so check that the course provides them.

A provider that answers these clearly is selling a legitimate piece of a training program. One that promises you'll be "fully OSHA certified" from a course alone is overselling.

## Using online training the right way

Online training works well as the **first third** of a complete program:

1. Operators complete the online formal instruction.
2. A qualified trainer runs hands-on practical training on your trucks.
3. The same trainer, or another qualified person, evaluates each operator in your workplace.
4. You certify each operator with the names and dates OSHA requires.

If you don't have someone in-house with the knowledge, training and experience to do steps 2 and 3, that's the part to bring in help for.
