"How do I get my forklift license?" is one of the most common questions we hear, and the honest answer surprises people: in the United States there is no government-issued forklift license. No state agency tests you, and no card from a website makes you legal to drive a truck at a new job.

What exists instead is **certification by the employer**, under OSHA's powered industrial truck standard, [29 CFR 1910.178(l)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-1910.178). The employer trains the operator, evaluates them on the job, and signs off. This guide walks through that process in order.

## Step 1: Confirm the operator is eligible

Federal child labor rules bar anyone under 18 from operating a forklift in non-agricultural work (Hazardous Occupations Order No. 7, 29 CFR 570.58). Beyond age, OSHA doesn't set a prerequisite. No prior experience is needed, because the training is what builds it.

## Step 2: Identify the trucks and the workplace

OSHA groups powered industrial trucks into seven classes. Training has to match the type of truck an operator will actually run, so start by listing what's on your floor:

- [Class 1](/classes/class-1-forklift): electric sit-down and stand-up rider forklifts
- [Class 2](/classes/class-2-forklift): narrow aisle reach trucks and order pickers
- [Class 3](/classes/class-3-forklift): electric pallet jacks, walkies and walkie-riders
- [Class 4](/classes/class-4-forklift) and [Class 5](/classes/class-5-forklift): propane, gas and diesel forklifts on cushion or pneumatic tires
- [Class 6](/classes/class-6-forklift): tow tractors and tuggers
- [Class 7](/classes/class-7-forklift): rough terrain forklifts and telehandlers

The standard also requires training on the **workplace**: surface conditions, ramps and slopes, pedestrian traffic, narrow aisles, loading docks, hazardous locations and ventilation where engine-powered trucks run indoors. An operator certified on a stand-up reach truck in a cold-storage warehouse isn't automatically ready for a rough terrain forklift on a construction site.

## Step 3: Formal instruction

The first part of training is formal instruction. OSHA lists lecture, discussion, interactive computer learning, video and written material as acceptable formats. This is where operators learn the truck's controls and instruments, its capacity and stability, the data plate, load handling, fueling or battery charging, and the daily inspection.

This is the only part that can be done in a classroom or online.

## Step 4: Practical, hands-on training

Next comes practical training: demonstrations by the trainer and exercises performed by the trainee, on the kind of truck they'll use. Until they finish, trainees may only operate under the direct supervision of someone qualified to train and evaluate them, and only where doing so doesn't put the trainee or other employees at risk.

## Step 5: The workplace evaluation

The operator then has to be **evaluated performing the job in the workplace** by a person with the knowledge, training and experience to judge their competence. This means watching them pick up and set down loads, travel with a load, maneuver around racking and people, and complete a pre-use inspection on their own truck.

## Step 6: The employer certifies

Once training and the evaluation are complete, the employer certifies it in writing. The certification must include:

- The operator's name
- The date of training
- The date of the evaluation
- The identity of the person or people who did the training and evaluation

Keep that record. It's what you'll show an OSHA compliance officer.

## Step 7: Keep it current

Certification isn't permanent. Each operator's performance has to be evaluated at least once every three years, and refresher training is required sooner after unsafe operation, an accident or near-miss, a poor evaluation, a new type of truck, or a change in the workplace. ## If you're looking for work, not hiring

Operators often ask how to get certified before they have a job. Because certification is tied to an employer's trucks and workplace, a new employer will still need to evaluate you and certify you for their equipment. OSHA does allow an employer to skip training topics you've already covered, provided you've been evaluated and found competent on their trucks. Prior training still counts for something: it shortens the process and shows you know the basics. But no course can hand you a certification that travels from job to job.
